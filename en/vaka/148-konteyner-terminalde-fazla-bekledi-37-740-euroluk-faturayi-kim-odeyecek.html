148 containers sat too long at the terminal: who pays the 37,740 euro bill?

In August one container in four ran past its free time. Customers are passing the demurrage on to Havenwijk; a third of the delay really was Havenwijk's doing.

Havenwijk Logistiek Logistics · Rotterdam, Netherlands · 96 employees · 42,000 square metres of warehouse, 28 tractor units

The problem

A container unloaded from a ship can stay at the terminal for five days free of charge. For every day after that the shipping line charges the importer 85 euros demurrage per container. In August Havenwijk collected 640 containers from the terminal on behalf of customers; 148 had gone past five days, by three days on average. The total bill was 37,740 euros, and customers were sending it on to Havenwijk: “You collected the container late.”

Operations Manager Marloes Hendriksen went through the 148 containers one by one. For 61, no suitable appointment could be found at the terminal. 38 had been held up by a customs inspection. In the remaining 49 the problem was ours: we were short of drivers because of summer holidays and had booked appointments late. The terminal released its appointment slots at midnight, three days ahead; by the time Havenwijk's planner started at seven in the morning, most of the daytime slots were taken. The night slots were empty, but Havenwijk's warehouse was closed at night.

According to Finance Manager Ruben Smeets, taking on the whole bill would swallow more than half of August's profit on container haulage. Customer and Contracts Manager Femke de Wilde checked the contracts: none of them said who owns the demurrage. Managing Director Pieter van Doorn went back and forth between the two extremes: refusing all of it would drive customers away, paying all of it would bring every future bill to Havenwijk.

By the numbers

Containers collected in August: 640; over the free time: 148 (23%)
Demurrage: 85 euros a day, average overrun 3 days, total 37,740 euros
Causes: 61 no terminal appointment, 49 Havenwijk's planning and driver shortage, 38 customs inspection
Contracts do not say who bears the demurrage
What would you do?

Would you pay the whole bill, or reject all of it? How would you work out your own share, and how would you explain it to the customer?

Suggested solution AI suggestion

1First 24 hours

  • Go to the customer not with a total but with the container list. Next to each container, show the day it came off the ship, the day the appointment was booked, the first slot available and the reason for the delay.
  • State your own share before the customer asks. If you are the first to say that 49 containers were down to you, they will believe what you say about the other 99.

2This week

  • Tie the three causes to three separate rules: the cost of delay from your own planning is yours, the cost of a customs inspection belongs to the owner of the goods. For what the terminal caused, neither you nor the customer should pay straight away.
  • Appeal to the shipping line for the containers with no terminal appointment available. If you hold the appointment records, you can ask for the free time to be extended; run the appeal yourself on the customer's behalf.
  • Assign someone to book appointments at the hour they are released. Phoning for a midnight release at seven in the morning means being seven hours late every day.
  • Work out what it would cost to use the night hours: two tractor units and one person in the warehouse. Set against it the demurrage you paid in a single month.

3Lasting fix

  • Write the sharing of demurrage into every contract. Discussing the rule after the invoice arrives means renegotiating every single time.
  • Record the reason for each container's delay that same day. A reason written up from memory at the month's end is no use either in an appeal or in a customer meeting.
  • List the containers whose free time is about to run out every morning; a container entering its fourth day should go to the top of the plan.
The lesson

A cost nobody has agreed to bear ends up with whoever is holding it last; the sharing rule is written before the invoice comes.
