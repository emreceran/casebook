A customer of thirty years files for insolvency: 412,000 euros owed, 38 half-finished gearboxes on the shop floor

A textile machinery maker that brings in 9% of revenue filed for insolvency in June. The insolvency administrator wants the unfinished order completed but makes no promises about the old debt.

Wendrath Antriebstechnik Manufacturing · Esslingen, Germany · 140 employees · 21 million euros in annual revenue

The problem

A textile machinery maker Wendrath had worked with for more than thirty years filed for insolvency on 9 June. At 1.89 million euros a year, the customer accounted for 9% of Wendrath's revenue. Unpaid invoices on the day of filing came to 412,000 euros. Wendrath had no credit insurance; Sabine Kollmer, the head of finance, had obtained a quote two years earlier, but the annual premium of 42,000 euros had been judged too expensive.

On the shop floor, 38 gearboxes for the same customer sat half-finished. By the calculation of Production Manager Jochen Brettschneider, 190,000 euros had been spent on them so far and another 70,000 euros was needed to finish them. Because the gearboxes were designed for the customer's machine, they could not be sold to anyone else. The court-appointed insolvency administrator was keeping the factory running and wanted the 38 gearboxes; as for the old 412,000 euros, he had only one answer: register it with the insolvency estate.

Sabine Kollmer wanted to stop deliveries altogether: “Let's not load goods onto a sinking ship.” Managing Director Henrik Wendrath thought the customer could be saved and that, if it was, it would remember who stood by it. Head of Sales Miriam Adler pointed out a detail: the 14 gearboxes from the last two shipments, goods worth 126,000 euros, might still be sitting unmounted in the customer's warehouse, and under Wendrath's terms of sale they remained Wendrath's property until paid for.

By the numbers

Unpaid invoices: 412,000 euros; the customer's share of revenue: 9%
38 half-finished gearboxes: 190,000 euros spent, 70,000 euros needed to finish, sale price 342,000 euros
Goods possibly still unmounted in the customer's warehouse: 14 gearboxes, 126,000 euros
No credit insurance; the premium turned down two years ago was 42,000 euros a year
What would you do?

Would you finish and deliver the 38 gearboxes, or stop production and cut your losses there? What would you do about the old debt?

Suggested solution AI suggestion

1First 24 hours

  • Don't mix up three separate matters: the debt from before the filing, the unpaid goods sitting in the customer's warehouse, and the half-finished work in your shop. Each has a different legal position and different bargaining power.
  • For the 14 gearboxes in the customer's warehouse, send the insolvency administrator written notice the same day: give the serial numbers, state that the goods are yours and that you do not consent to their use. Once a gearbox is mounted in a machine, you can't go after it.
  • On the 38 gearboxes in your shop, stop work for a day and itemise everything spent so far. Make the decision on figures, not estimates.

2This week

  • Negotiate a separate agreement with the administrator for new deliveries. Deliveries made after the filing with his approval are paid as priority costs and don't go into the estate; even so, make payment on delivery a condition.
  • Work out whether to finish the 38 gearboxes by asking the right question: the 190,000 euros is spent and won't come back. The question is whether it makes sense to spend another 70,000 euros to receive 342,000 euros.
  • Ask for an on-site count of the 14 gearboxes in the warehouse. Take back the unused ones or ask to be paid for them up front; the administrator needs those gearboxes to keep production going.
  • Register the old debt with the insolvency estate within the deadline and set aside a provision for the full amount. Whatever comes from there goes down as a pleasant surprise, not into the budget.

3Lasting fix

  • Set an open-receivables limit for each customer. Once the limit is exceeded, a new shipment goes out only with the joint signature of the managing director and the head of finance.
  • Reassess credit insurance by looking not at the premium but at what it would cost if a single customer failed.
  • On custom work that can't be sold to anyone else, take an advance with the order so that the risk on semi-finished goods isn't yours alone.
The lesson

Work with a failed customer doesn't end, the books just split in two: yesterday's debt waits in the estate, today's delivery is paid for up front.
