The landlord wants to raise the first location's rent by 42%, and the lease ends at year's end

The rent on Copperwren's most profitable location would go from $7,400 to $10,500. The roaster is in this shop too; moving would cost $295,000.

Copperwren Coffee Food & Drink · Portland, USA · 41 employees · 4 locations, $2.6 million in annual revenue

The problem

The lease on the first location Copperwren Coffee opened twelve years ago ends on December 31. In July the landlord announced the new number: the rent, now $7,400 a month, will go to $10,500. That's a 42% increase, or $37,200 a year. The landlord says a big chain wants the space too.

This location does $840,000 a year in revenue and contributes $118,000 to the company's profit; the new rent would take nearly a third of that. In the back of the shop sits the machine that roasts the coffee for all four locations and the wholesale accounts. Finance Director Ruth Calloway ran the numbers on moving: there's a space 600 meters away for $6,800 a month, but the move, the build-out and new ventilation for the roaster would cost $295,000.

Founder Hannah Whitlock is furious: “That corner was empty when we got here. We brought the street back to life, and now they're sending us the bill for it.” Her first instinct is to accept the rent and raise prices at this location. Director of Locations Eli Navarro worries that a price increase will cost them customers. Ruth Calloway points out that nobody knows what the landlord's number is based on; no one has ever looked at what the other shops on the block pay.

Suggested solution AI suggestion

1First 24 hours

  • Don't tell the landlord yes or no; ask for time in writing. Before you answer, there are two numbers you need: comparable rents on the street and the real cost of moving.
  • Spread the cost of the move over the years. If you spend $295,000 to save $3,700 a month in rent, it takes you more than six years to get your money back. That also shows how strong your hand is in the negotiation: not very.

2This week

  • Find out what similar shops on the street pay. A commercial lease consultant can do this in a few days; against the landlord's number you'll have a list in your hands, not a feeling.
  • Ask what the landlord wants. A high rent may not be the only thing he's after: a long-term tenant, a well-kept storefront and a space that doesn't sit empty are worth money to him too.
  • When you ask for a break on rent, give something in return: a longer lease, a set annual increase, a contribution to a facade renovation.
  • Take the chain threat seriously, but don't overblow it. For a chain to take over the space, renovate and open takes months, and during those months the landlord collects no rent.

3Lasting fix

  • Put the lease end dates for all four locations on a single calendar and start talks on each one 18 months before it ends.
  • Separate roasting from retail. As long as your roaster sits behind a shop, that shop's landlord is the landlord of the whole company.
  • Once a year, calculate each location's rent as a share of its revenue; if the ratio is climbing, you'll find out before the lease ends.
The lesson

A tenant who sits down to negotiate rent only months before the lease ends sits down with the weakest card in hand.
