A 40-store chain wants 96,000 bars of soap a year, and the drying racks have room for 50,000

A chain of natural-products stores wants to stock Clairvent soaps in all its shops. The price on offer is a quarter of the profit on the website, and the quantity asked for is beyond the workshop's capacity.

Savonnerie Clairvent E-commerce · Lyon, France · 14 employees · 62,000 orders a year

The problem

In January a natural-products chain with 40 stores in France wrote to Clairvent: it wanted to sell the soaps in all its shops. The offer was 96,000 bars a year at 3.40 euros a bar. The chain would pay on 60-day terms and be able to return unsold product. For founder Camille Vasseur this was the moment she had waited ten years for: for the first time the brand would leave the internet and appear on a shelf.

Thibault Renaudin, Head of Production, counted the racks first. Once cold-process soap is poured into the mould, it spends five weeks on a drying rack, and that time can't be shortened. The racks hold at most 260,000 bars a year; the workshop already makes 210,000. That leaves room for 50,000 bars, and the chain wants 96,000. A second drying room would mean an 85,000 euro investment and two new employees; two salaries come to 68,000 euros a year.

Nadia Belkacem, Head of E-commerce, showed the other side of the sum. A bar costs 2.60 euros to make. Every bar sold to the chain leaves 0.80 euros; the same bar sold on the website leaves 3.10 euros. The chain's annual order would bring 76,800 euros in contribution, but in the first year the investment and salaries would take twice that. And there was one more question: what would happen if website customers started buying the soap from the shop next door?

By the numbers

The chain's offer: 96,000 bars a year, 3.40 euros a bar, 60-day terms, right to return
Drying capacity 260,000 bars, current production 210,000; free space 50,000 bars
Contribution per bar: 0.80 euros through the chain, 3.10 euros on the website
Cost of raising capacity: 85,000 euros investment, 68,000 euros a year in salaries

Suggested solution AI suggestion

1First 24 hours

  • Before saying yes or no to the offer, break it into its parts: quantity, price, terms, returns. You don't know which of them the chain insists on; if you treat it all as one package, there is nothing left to negotiate.
  • Explain your capacity with numbers, not words. The five-week drying time isn't your whim, it is the product itself; the chain knows that is why the soap is in demand.

2This week

  • Prepare a counter-offer that fits your free capacity: 10 to 12 stores in one region instead of 40, a few best-selling varieties instead of the whole range, six months instead of a year.
  • Don't accept the right to return. For a small producer, soap coming back is both a stock problem and a cash problem; instead, keep the first order small and renew it according to sales.
  • Renegotiate the price and terms on a small quantity. The price for 96,000 bars doesn't have to be the same as the price for 20,000.
  • Decide up front what you will measure during the trial: sales per store and the trend of website orders in the cities where the stores are. Within six months you'll see whether the stores eat into the site or feed it.

3Lasting fix

  • Cap the share of production that any one buyer can take. The customer you grow your capacity for will be the one setting the price a year later.
  • Make the investment decision on actual sales, not on the promise of an order. Talk about the second drying room after the trial results.
  • Put store and website prices under a written rule from the start, so the channels don't undercut each other.
The lesson

The answer to a big offer doesn't have to be yes or no; often the best answer is a smaller offer written to your own size.
