A customer whose card had expired was locked out of their account three days later

Every month 236 subscribers' payments failed and 86 of them had their accounts locked. 19 of those locked out never came back; most hadn't wanted to leave.

Tallowfield Software Software · Leeds, United Kingdom · 46 employees · 3,800 subscribing businesses

The problem

At Tallowfield the subscription fee is taken automatically from a card or bank account each month. Last year, on average 236 subscribers' payments failed each month. Under a rule written in the company's early years, an account whose payment wasn't fixed within three days was locked. Of the 236 subscribers, 150 paid within three days and 86 had their accounts locked. Of those, 67 paid and got their account reopened, and 19 never came back.

Callum Hartigan, Head of Customer Support, knew that locked accounts brought his team 140 calls a month, but it was a single conversation that spurred him into action. An electrician had found his account locked while preparing a quote in a customer's kitchen; his card had been renewed that week. When we looked at why the payments failed, the picture was clear: 97 were expired or reissued cards, 88 were insufficient funds, and 51 were bank declines and other causes.

At that time an average of 65 subscribers a month were leaving; 19 of them, or 29%, had not actually decided to leave but had dropped off because their payment snagged. Callum Hartigan wanted the lock removed. Finance Director Deborah Ainscough objected: “Without the lock nobody pays. It's the only way to remind a small business about money.” Founder Graham Pellow, having written the rule himself, wasn't very keen to change it either.

By the numbers

Subscribers with a failed payment each month: 236 (6.2% of 3,800 subscribers)
150 paid within three days, 86 locked out; 19 of the locked-out never returned
Causes: 97 expired cards, 88 insufficient funds, 51 bank declines and other
Support calls caused by the lock: 140 a month
What would you do?

Would you lock the account of a customer who doesn't pay? If you didn't lock it, how would you get the payment?

Suggested solution AI suggestion

1First 24 hours

  • Tell apart the customer whose payment failed from the customer who doesn't want to pay. A tradesperson whose card has expired isn't in debt, they just don't know; they need a reminder, not a penalty.
  • Look at how many of the 86 locked accounts paid afterwards. If most of them pay, the lock isn't getting payments in, it's just stopping the customer in the middle of a job.

2This week

  • Extend the three-day period and fill the gap: retry the payment on different days, and warn by email and text message before each attempt.
  • Replace the lock with a graded restriction. When the period runs out, the customer can keep viewing their records and finishing the job in hand, but can't open new ones.
  • For cards that are about to expire, send a warning a month before the date. 97 of the 236 failed payments come from a cause that is known before it happens.
  • Have a person call customers who are nearing the end of the period. Twenty or thirty calls a month is a small job next to the 19 subscribers being lost.

3Lasting fix

  • Track cancellations in two separate lines: those who leave of their own choice and those who drop off because their payment snagged. The remedies for the two are nothing alike.
  • Review old rules once a year. The three-day lock was written when we had 400 customers; nobody had looked at what the same rule produced with 3,800.
  • On collection rules, put the finance and support teams' figures side by side: time to collect, amount uncollected, subscribers lost, calls received.
The lesson

A customer whose payment snagged and a customer who won't pay are not the same person; a company that applies the same rule to both shows the door to the one who wanted to stay.
