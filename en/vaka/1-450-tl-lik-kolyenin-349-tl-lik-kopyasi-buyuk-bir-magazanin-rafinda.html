A 349 TL copy of the 1,450 TL necklace is on a big retailer's shelf

A plated copy of the design that brings in 40% of revenue has gone on sale at a store chain. Sales fell by nearly half in three weeks; the design is not registered.

Atölye Nar E-commerce · Izmir · 12 employees · 1,900 orders a month

The problem

Atölye Nar's best seller is the Nar Tanesi necklace, handcrafted from 925 sterling silver. It is priced at 1,450 TL, sells about 520 a month, and with 754,000 TL brings in 40% of revenue. A customer sent a photo and the company found out: a large store chain is selling a plated-metal copy of the same design for 349 TL.

Weekly sales were around 130 units; after the copy appeared, they fell to 97, then 81, and last week 68. Founder Zeynep Narin drew the design three years ago but never registered it. The lawyer wants 25,000 TL for a cease-and-desist letter; he says that if it goes to court it could take a year or two and the costs could reach 180,000 TL.

Zeynep Narin wants to call out the store by name on social media: “Let 212,000 people see.” Social Media Lead Aslı Yücel is hesitant; an accusation made before the evidence is solid could turn into a countersuit. Workshop Lead Deniz Akça proposes bringing out a slimmer, 890 TL version of the necklace and answering on price.

What would you do?

Would you expose the store in front of your followers, file a lawsuit, or cut your price?

Suggested solution AI suggestion

1First 24 hours

  • Gather evidence before posting anything: the design's first sketches, the date of the first post, the first sales invoice. Buy the product in the store, keep the receipt, and have a notary document it.
  • Leave the angrily written post in drafts. An accusation that names the store, with no registration in hand, can put you in the position of defendant.

2This week

  • Send a cease-and-desist letter through a lawyer and write directly to the chain's purchasing department the same day. Big chains often buy a ready-made product from a supplier; they may hear from you for the first time that it is a copy.
  • Don't cut the price. You can't compete on price with a 349 TL plated piece; where you compete is silver, handwork and the designer's signature.
  • Tell a story instead of making accusations: show how the necklace is made in the workshop, how many hours it takes, whose hands it comes from. Make the difference visible without mentioning the copy.
  • Add a numbered, signed certificate to every necklace. A mark that separates the original from the copy gives both the customer and the person they give it to as a gift something to say.

3Lasting fix

  • Register your most important designs that are on sale, and every design that comes after, before putting them on the market.
  • Track the revenue share of any single design. A brand that gets 40% from one product shrinks by half the day that product is copied.
  • Release at least two strong new designs a year. The copycat always follows your previous design.
The lesson

Protecting a design you haven't registered always costs more than registering it.
