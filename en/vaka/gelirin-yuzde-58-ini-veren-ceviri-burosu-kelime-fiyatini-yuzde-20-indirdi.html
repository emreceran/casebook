The translation agency that provides 58% of her income cut its per-word rate by 20%

The agency announced that it will run texts through machine translation first and send them out for post-editing, paying 1.20 TL per word instead of 1.50 TL. Of Selin Karaduman's monthly income of 168,000 TL, 97,500 TL comes from this agency.

Selin Karaduman Çeviri Self-employed · Izmir · 1 employees · 95,000 words a month

The problem

Selin Karaduman has worked with a large translation agency for six years. In July she earned 168,000 TL: 97,500 TL from the agency, 46,500 TL from her two clients abroad, and 24,000 TL from her own clients in Turkey. The agency's share is 58%. The agency sends her 65,000 words a month and pays 1.50 TL per word.

On August 10, an email came from the agency: starting September 1, texts will go through machine translation first, translators will do the post-editing, and the rate will be 1.20 TL per word. For the same word count, that means 19,500 TL less a month. The email went to all the translators with the same text; it left no room to negotiate.

In Selin Karaduman's view, post-editing really is faster for technical manuals. In contracts, though, finding a term the machine chose wrong takes longer than translating the text from scratch, and 40% of the work the agency sends is contracts. If she refuses, more than half her income is gone in a day. If she accepts, she'll have to work more to earn the same money, and next year she'll get a new copy of the same email.

By the numbers

July income: 168,000 TL; the translation agency's share 97,500 TL (58%)
Work the agency sends: 65,000 words a month, 40% of it contracts
Per-word rate drops from 1.50 TL to 1.20 TL
Monthly loss for the same work: 19,500 TL
What would you do?

Would you accept the new rate, leave the agency, or split the work by type and negotiate?

Suggested solution AI suggestion

1First 24 hours

  • Don't reply to the email the same day. First split the last three months' work by type: how many words of manuals, how many of contracts, and in which does post-editing really save time?
  • Run a test on a contract text: take two sections of the same length, translate one from scratch, post-edit the other from machine output, and time it. Take the agency a measurement, not an opinion.

2This week

  • Don't say “no” to the agency, make a counteroffer: the new rate is accepted for manuals, and the old rate stays for contracts. An agency doesn't want to lose a good legal translator over 30 kuruş.
  • Put a cap on the number of words you set aside for the agency. Don't try to close the gap by doing more of the work you've accepted the cut on.
  • Give the time that opens up to your own clients. Write to everyone who has given you work even once in the last two years; remind the old ones before you go looking for new ones.
  • Call the places that buy contract translation directly: law firms, exporting manufacturers. The price the agency gets from them is nearly double what you get.

3Lasting fix

  • Put a limit on any one client's share of your income and check it every month. The client who provides 58% also sets your price.
  • Set aside a few hours a week for new clients when work is plentiful, not when it's slow. Whoever starts looking when work drops off is already late.
  • Make your specialty visible. A person known as a “contract and machinery manual translator” instead of just a “translator” is chosen for the work, not the per-word rate.
The lesson

A freelancer who gets more than half their income from one place is that place's employee; they just lack the rights.
