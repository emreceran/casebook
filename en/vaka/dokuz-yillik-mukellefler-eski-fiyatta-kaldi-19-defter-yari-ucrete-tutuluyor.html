Nine-year clients stuck at the old price: 19 sets of books kept at half the fee

The 19 oldest clients pay an average of 3,400 TL a month; a new client with the same workload pays 7,100 TL. Nurcan Balkış has shied away from raising these clients' fees for years.

Nurcan Balkış Mali Müşavirlik Self-employed · Konya · 2 employees · 62 clients

The problem

Nineteen of Nurcan Balkış's 62 clients have been with her for nine years or longer. Every year when fees were updated, she gave these clients a small increase as “old friends,” and in some years none at all. Today the average fee for those 19 clients is 3,400 TL a month. Clients of similar size who came in over the last two years pay 7,100 TL.

Over one month, intern Büşra Altınok noted how many hours went to each client. The 19 old clients take 34% of the office's working hours and bring in 17% of its revenue. The ones who call most, bring their invoices on the last day, and ask for bank letters for loan applications are mostly them, too.

The gap is 3,700 TL per client per month, 70,300 TL in total, or 843,600 TL a year. Nurcan Balkış is afraid that if she raises fees, the clients will leave. There are colleagues in the market who keep books for less, and most of these clients are people who have also referred other clients to her.

What would you do?

Would you tell an old client the real price all at once, phase it in, or keep the old price out of loyalty?

Suggested solution AI suggestion

1First 24 hours

  • First look at the numbers yourself. For each of the 19 clients, write down three things: how many invoices are processed a month, how many employees' payroll is done, and how many returns are filed. The fee conversation is held with this sheet of paper, not with the line “everything has gotten more expensive.”
  • Next to it, write what a new client with the same workload pays. You'll see who is paying for the discount you give the old client: you.

2This week

  • Don't announce the increase by message. Meet each of the 19 clients face to face; someone who has been with you for years shouldn't learn this from a letter.
  • Don't close the gap in one go. Going from 3,400 TL to 7,100 TL is an increase of more than 100%; split it into two steps and give the date of the second step today.
  • Give the old client something, but not on price: for example, the fee stays fixed through the year, or a one-hour status review at the year-end meeting.
  • Be ready for the one who will leave. A proper handover of the books is part of your reputation, and a departing client will talk about you around the market.

3Lasting fix

  • Set fees by workload, not by person: number of invoices, number of employees, number of returns. Make the table the same for everyone.
  • Update fees every year in the same month, for all clients together. A raise postponed client by client turns into half price nine years later.
  • Keep time records for two months a year. Without knowing how many hours each client takes, you can't know which one is profitable.
The lesson

A raise withheld from an old client for years looks like a debt of loyalty, but only you pay the bill for it, every month.
