A customer halved its stock, and 3,300 of the 6,200 pallet positions set aside for it are empty

The contract charges per pallet position used, with no minimum commitment. In eight months the customer went from 6,200 pallets to 2,900; the racking still stands reserved in its name.

Havenwijk Logistiek Logistics · Rotterdam, Netherlands · 96 employees · 42,000 square metres of warehouse, 28 tractor units

The problem

Since 2019 Havenwijk has run the warehouse of an online shop selling home and garden products. One part of the warehouse, with 6,200 pallet positions, is reserved for this customer; the racking was built to suit its products. Under the contract the customer pays 4.20 euros a week for every pallet position it uses. In January all 6,200 positions were full and weekly storage revenue was 26,040 euros.

At the start of the year the customer's new supply chain director changed the stock policy: more frequent, smaller shipments from suppliers. The positions in use fell every month and by August were down to 2,900. Weekly revenue dropped to 12,180 euros; the gap of 13,860 euros a week comes to 720,000 euros a year. By Finance Manager Ruben Smeets's calculation, the rent on that section and the share of racking investment come to 14,500 euros a week; storage was now making a loss. Order-picking revenue had not changed.

The customer was not in breach of contract; no minimum commitment had ever been written in, because in 2019 the warehouse was always full. Managing Director Pieter van Doorn did not want to lean on a customer that brings in 21% of revenue. Customer and Contracts Manager Femke de Wilde wanted to give the empty racking to someone else, but the customer regards that section as its own space and said it could fill it again in the peak season.

By the numbers

Pallet positions reserved for the customer: 6,200; in use in August: 2,900
Weekly storage revenue: from 26,040 euros to 12,180 euros
Weekly fixed cost of the section: 14,500 euros
No minimum commitment in the contract; the customer's share of revenue is 21%
What would you do?

Would you tell your biggest customer “either commit or give up the racking”? If you don't, who pays for the empty racking?

Suggested solution AI suggestion

1First 24 hours

  • Go to the customer not with a complaint but with the sums: the weekly cost of the section reserved for them and the revenue it brings in today. While cutting stock, the customer most likely never saw what it meant on your side.
  • Don't argue with their decision. Cutting stock may be right for them; what needs to be discussed is in whose name the freed space is sitting empty.

2This week

  • Offer the customer two options: either they commit to a set number of pallet positions and that space stays reserved for them in the peak season too, or they pay only for what they use but with no guarantee of space.
  • Derive the commitment figure from their data: the lowest and highest stock during the year. If they need space in the peak season, a commitment suits them as well.
  • Look at who could fill the racking that is being freed. Firms that import seasonal goods and need warehouse space for part of the year are good candidates for this kind of space.
  • Before giving the racking to a new customer, draw the physical boundary. If two customers' goods get mixed in the same aisle, you lose the rent you've won in stock-count differences.

3Lasting fix

  • For every area reserved for a specific customer, write a minimum commitment into the contract. If you set the space aside, you shouldn't carry the risk of it standing empty alone.
  • Hold a stock forecast meeting with major customers twice a year. Learn about a change in stock policy from the customer, not from the invoice.
  • Track the revenue and cost of each section separately. While a customer's total revenue looks good, a single line can be losing money for months.
The lesson

If you set the space aside and get paid only as it is used, you pay the price of every efficiency decision the customer makes.
