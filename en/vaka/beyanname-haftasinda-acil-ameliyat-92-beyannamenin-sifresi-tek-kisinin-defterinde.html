Emergency surgery in tax-return week: passwords for 92 returns in one person's notebook

Nurcan Balkış went into surgery on August 19. Ninety-two returns were due within nine days, and she was the only one who could log in to the system and sign them.

Nurcan Balkış Mali Müşavirlik Self-employed · Konya · 2 employees · 62 clients

The problem

On the night of August 19, Nurcan Balkış was operated on for appendicitis. The doctor ordered ten days of rest. The calendar wouldn't wait: by the 26th, 38 withholding and social security premium and service returns were due, and by the 28th, 54 value-added tax returns. That made 92 returns in nine days.

About 70% of the records had been processed. Intern Büşra Altınok could enter the rest, but she has no authority to submit returns. The password for the return filing system and the clients' bank and agency passwords were in the notebook in Nurcan Balkış's drawer and in her head. Which client always brings which invoice on the last day was also known only to her.

She had three paths. Work from the hospital bed on a laptop; the doctor wouldn't allow it. Hand the work to a colleague; she was far from comfortable with that, since she would be introducing her clients to them. Or file the returns late; then the penalties would fall on the clients, and they would hold her to account.

By the numbers

Returns to be filed in nine days: 92 (38 withholding, 54 value-added tax)
Share of records processed on the day of surgery: about 70%
People authorized to submit returns: 1
Rest prescribed by the doctor: 10 days
What would you do?

Would you try working from the hospital, entrust your clients to a colleague, or risk the penalty for filing late?

Suggested solution AI suggestion

1First 24 hours

  • Call a colleague you trust today. The fear of losing your client is real, but 92 late returns will cost you more clients than that.
  • Put the arrangement in writing: the scope of work, the fee per return, and a commitment not to take over clients. Even if it's two lines, make it signed; that's how friendships stay protected, too.
  • Give the intern a single list: whose records are finished, who is still waiting on invoices, whose phone needs to be called. If the order in your head doesn't get onto paper, no one can carry on.

2This week

  • Tell the clients briefly what's happening. A sentence like “I've had surgery, and your return will be filed under the supervision of my colleague” won't lose their trust; an unannounced delay will.
  • Give the approval yourself and let someone else do the work. See and approve each return's summary total by phone; you don't have to sit at a screen.
  • Call the clients with missing invoices on the first day. The work you leave to the last day when you're healthy can't be left to the last day when you're sick.
  • If anything is late, don't pass the penalty on to the client. The delay came from the way your office is set up.

3Lasting fix

  • Take the passwords out of the notebook, move them to a password manager, and write down who can get access, and how, in an emergency.
  • Make a reciprocal cover agreement with one or two colleagues: if one of you falls ill, the other keeps the work running, on terms agreed in advance.
  • Prepare a one-page work card for each client: their returns, their habits, who to call. The office should live in the file, not in your head.
The lesson

Someone who works alone can't be their own backup; who the backup will be is decided before getting sick.
