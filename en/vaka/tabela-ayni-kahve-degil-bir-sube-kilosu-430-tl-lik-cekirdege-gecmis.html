Same sign, different coffee: a branch has switched to 430 TL a kilo beans

A licensed branch is using cheaper beans instead of the ones it buys from headquarters at 780 TL. Customers have started writing that the taste has changed.

Demli Kahve Food & Drink · Ankara · 80 employees · 9 branches

The problem

One of the five branches to which Demli Kahve has licensed its name has been drawing attention for six weeks. Its online rating fell from 4.6 to 4.1; 38 reviews say “the taste has changed.” Operations and Quality Manager Esra Kurtuluş looked at the records: the branch sells about 17,000 cups a month, which requires 310 kilograms of beans. Its purchases from headquarters have dropped from 310 kilograms to 120.

On an unannounced visit, sacks from another roaster were found in the storeroom. The operator didn't deny it: headquarters sells beans at 780 TL a kilo, and he finds them for 430 TL. On 190 kilograms, he makes 66,500 TL a month. His defense is ready too: his rent has gone up 60% in a year; and besides, he knows that headquarters costs the beans at 640 TL and sells them with a 22% markup. “You take 6% of revenue, and you also make money on the beans,” he says.

Founder Alper Sönmez wants to terminate the contract and take down the sign: “Other coffee can't be sold under my name.” Finance Manager Cihan Bulut is more cautious: the branch does 1.9 million TL in monthly revenue and pays headquarters 114,000 TL in license fees; he also thinks the operator isn't entirely wrong about the price of beans. The contract says the beans must be bought from headquarters, but the penalty for a breach isn't clear.

What would you do?

Would you terminate the contract and take down the sign, or would you also put on the table the point on which the operator is right?

Suggested solution AI suggestion

1First 24 hours

  • Meet the operator face to face and separate the two issues: selling other beans under the brand name is not negotiable and ends today. The bean price can be discussed, but at a separate table.
  • Document the situation with a record and issue a written warning: within 15 days, the branch must return fully to headquarters' beans. Make the termination decision with documents, not with anger.
  • Compare the other four operator branches' bean purchases with the number of cups sold today. If one branch did this, the question is: was it only that one?

2This week

  • Look honestly at your own math. If you take both a revenue share and bean profit from the operator, you have also built the incentive that pushes him toward cheap beans.
  • Calculate lowering headquarters' margin on beans: if 10% is applied instead of 22%, the price drops to 704 TL. What does that cost headquarters across five branches, and what does the contraband coffee cost the brand?
  • Make a quiet makeup to the branch's customers: two weeks of tastings of freshly roasted beans. The best way to announce the taste is back is to let them taste it.
  • Bring the five operators together in the same meeting. Explain both the rule and the price change to all of them at once, so it doesn't look like one branch got a concession.

3Lasting fix

  • Write explicit penalties into the contracts: a written warning and inspection costs for the first breach, termination for the second.
  • Automatically compare bean purchases with cups rung up at the register every month; a deviation of more than 10% should generate an alert.
  • Base headquarters' income more on revenue share than on bean profit. The more good coffee the operator sells, the more you both win.
The lesson

If you've built a system that makes breaking the rule profitable, oversight alone is not enough.
