The plant that takes the pomace stopped at 90 tonnes a day, while the mill produces 200 tonnes a day

At the start of the harvest, the pomace plant limited intake and asked for a fee instead of paying. The pond holds 1,500 tonnes; if it fills, the mill stops and olives wait on trailers.

Oleícola Montesira Food · Jaén, Spain · 38 employees · 412 farmer members, 2,400–3,100 tonnes of olive oil a year

The problem

After olives are pressed, far more waste is left than oil: about 80% of the olives' weight comes out as a wet pulp called pomace. For years Montesira had been giving this pomace to a processing plant 40 kilometres away, which paid 4 euros a tonne. In November 2025, as the harvest was starting, the plant announced new terms: because its drying capacity was full, it would take at most 90 tonnes a day from Montesira and would no longer pay, instead charging a processing fee of 7 euros a tonne.

Of the 12,000 tonnes of olives expected that season, 9,600 tonnes of pomace would come out. The 11 euro a tonne difference meant 105,600 euros over the season. But what really worried Andrés Cobo, Head of Mill and Quality, was the cap on quantity. In the five busiest weeks of the harvest, 200 tonnes of pomace a day came out of the mill. If the plant took 90 tonnes, 110 tonnes would pile up in the pond every day, and the 1,500-tonne pond would overflow in under two weeks. Of the 7,000 tonnes coming out in five weeks, there was no room for 2,350 tonnes.

Managing Director Rafael Molinero thought of asking members to spread out the harvest. Inmaculada Serrano, Head of Finance and Member Relations, was against it: members had arranged their picking crews weeks earlier, and olives waiting on the branch lose quality. An environmental permit for a second pond would take months. There was another pomace plant 140 kilometres away, but shipping there cost 14 euros a tonne.

By the numbers

Pomace expected in the season: 9,600 tonnes; old terms bring 4 euros a tonne in income, new terms cost 7 euros a tonne
Pomace in the busy five weeks: 200 tonnes a day, 7,000 tonnes in total
The plant will take: 90 tonnes a day, 3,150 tonnes in five weeks; pond capacity 1,500 tonnes
Left without a place: 2,350 tonnes; shipping to the second, distant plant 14 euros a tonne
What would you do?

Would you ask members to slow down the harvest, pay for the expensive shipping, or sit down and negotiate with the plant?

Suggested solution AI suggestion

1First 24 hours

  • Work out the day first: on which day does the pond fill? The 105,600 euro part of the problem is a matter for negotiation, but the day the mill stops is a matter of the calendar. The second can't wait.
  • Empty the pond completely before the harvest starts. A 1,500-tonne buffer is only 1,500 tonnes if you enter the season with it empty.

2This week

  • Make a deal with the plant 140 kilometres away for the busy weeks only. You don't have to send all the pomace there; the part of the 110-tonne daily surplus that doesn't fit in the pond is enough.
  • Lock in the shipping before the season starts. Looking for trucks in the middle of the harvest leaves both the price and the truck to someone else's mercy.
  • With the nearby plant, discuss not quantity but the calendar: outside the busy five weeks, can it take more than 90 tonnes a day? You work off the pomace that has built up in the pond once the season slows.
  • Explain the situation to members, but don't ask them to postpone the harvest. The quality loss of olives waiting on the branch costs more than shipping the pomace.

3Lasting fix

  • Manage pomace not as waste but as a line item with its own accounts: at the start of the season, quantity, buyer, price and a backup buyer all in writing.
  • Look at ways to cut the amount of pomace at the mill: separating the stones and selling them as fuel both reduces volume and brings income.
  • Don't depend on a single buyer. Sign multi-year contracts with two plants so that when one's capacity is full, the other is ready.
The lesson

What stops production is often not the product itself; it's the by-product nobody owns, which locks up the whole line once it has nowhere to go.
