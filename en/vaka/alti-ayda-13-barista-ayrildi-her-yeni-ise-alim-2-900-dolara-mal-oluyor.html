13 baristas left in six months: every new hire costs $2,900

Thirteen of Copperwren's 29 baristas quit between December and May. The founder wants to raise the hourly wage; most of the people who left complained about something else.

Copperwren Coffee Food & Drink · Portland, USA · 41 employees · 4 locations, $2.6 million in annual revenue

The problem

Copperwren Coffee has 29 baristas across its four locations. In the six months from December to May, 13 of them left. A new barista costs the company $2,900 for the posting, the interview, and the three weeks before they can work the counter alone, with an experienced person working alongside. The six-month bill comes to $37,700.

Founder Hannah Whitlock sees the reason in pay. Baristas earn $17 an hour, and with tips average $23.50. Hannah Whitlock wants to raise the hourly wage by $1.50. Finance Director Ruth Calloway worked out the math: the baristas work a total of 42,200 hours a year, and the raise would cost about $63,300 a year. That's more than one and a half times what the departures cost over six months, and there's no guarantee the raise would stop people from leaving.

Director of Locations Eli Navarro talked to 11 of the 13 who left. Six complained about the work schedule: the weekly schedule is posted three days ahead, and hours swing from 18 to 34 from one week to the next. Three said pay. Two had left for school or because they were moving. The location managers, though, are against posting the schedule earlier; since sales change with the weather, they want to wait until the last day.

By the numbers

Suggested solution AI suggestion

1First 24 hours

  • Put off the raise decision and look at what the people who left said. If six out of eleven cited the schedule and not the money, the $63,300 raise would be an expensive answer to the wrong question.
  • Ask the baristas who work for you now the same question. What the person who left said is what the person who stayed is thinking too; they just haven't left yet.

2This week

  • Post the schedule two weeks ahead. If you need to fine-tune for the weather, do it by adding hours, not cutting hours.
  • Guarantee a weekly minimum of hours to any barista who wants it. Someone whose income swings by half from one week to the next can't plan for rent and takes the first steady job.
  • Leave shift swaps to the baristas: let them work it out among themselves, and the location manager just approves.
  • Raise pay by tenure, not for everyone at once. Those who complete six months and one year should get more; the expensive one isn't the new hire coming in, it's the experienced barista going out.

3Lasting fix

  • Write down the number of departures and the reason every month. Thirteen people in six months comes out to two a month, and looked at one by one it seems normal.
  • Put how tips are split on a chart everyone at the location can see. Any money that's unclear gets told as unfairness.
  • Show baristas a path forward: trainer, shift lead, roasting. Someone who knows they'll be doing the same job at the same counter two years from now won't stay two years.
The lesson

When an employee leaves, they say “pay”; much of the time, what they mean is not knowing how much they'll earn next week.
