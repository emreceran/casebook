E-commerceOctober 3, 2026

One post went viral, orders tripled, and delivery times stretched to three weeks

Atölye Nar E-commerce · Izmir · 12 employees · 1,900 orders a month
The problem

The workshop, which gets 440 orders a week, received 3,790 orders in three weeks. Capacity is 520 pieces a week; the founder works 15 hours a day and every job passes through her.

How it was solved

It did not stop orders; it introduced a weekly quota and a waiting list and handed the prep work to temporary workers. The backlog melted away in five weeks, and delivery time fell to 6 days.

What would you do? 3 comments ↓

What happened?

A photo of a well-known actress wearing an Atölye Nar necklace was passed from hand to hand on social media. Orders, which averaged 440 a week, suddenly jumped to 1,320; the next two weeks brought 1,290 and 1,180 orders. The workshop's capacity is 520 pieces a week. Of the 3,790 orders received in three weeks, 1,560 were made and shipped, 80 were canceled, and 2,150 are waiting. Delivery time stretched from 4 days to 21.

Founder Zeynep Narin works 15 hours a day. Design is her job, but she also does the final check on every piece, writes a handwritten note for every package, makes the supplier payments, and answers the difficult customer messages. 1,100 unanswered messages have piled up in the inbox; most ask the same question: where is my order?

Workshop Lead Deniz Akça proposes having part of production done by an outside workshop. Zeynep Narin is against it: “People buy this because it is handmade and comes from our hands.” Orders Lead Bora Sezgin's suggestion is harsher: stop taking orders. Zeynep Narin won't agree to that either; she fears this level of interest will never come again.

By the numbers

Weekly orders rose from 440 to 1,320
Workshop capacity: 520 pieces a week
2,150 orders waiting, 1,100 unanswered messages
Delivery time stretched from 4 days to 21

What did Atölye Nar do?

The same day, a message with the estimated shipping date and the refund option went to the 2,150 waiting customers; 240 asked for refunds and 1,910 waited. A weekly quota of 400 orders was set on the site, and the waiting list kicked in once it filled. Attaching chains, polishing and boxing went to three temporary workers, and messages went to Aslı Yücel and an assistant. The craftspeople went back to engraving only, and capacity rose from 520 pieces a week to 780.

With 780 pieces produced a week and 400 new orders taken, the backlog shrank by 380 every week and was gone in five weeks. Delivery time fell from 21 days to 6. Deniz Akça took over final quality control; Zeynep Narin now looks only at pieces picked at random from each batch, and her working day dropped from 15 hours to 9. The waiting list grew to 3,400 people.

What to do in a similar situation, step by step

1First 24 hours

  • Change the delivery time on the site and the account to the real figure today. A customer who orders knowing they will wait three weeks is patient; a customer who expects four days and waits three weeks cancels and writes about it.
  • Send a single message to all 2,150 waiting customers: your order is in the queue, the estimated shipping date is this, and if you don't want to wait, we'll refund you right away. That one message will close most of the 1,100 messages.
  • Write down one day in the founder's life: which work can only she do? Design and final quality approval. The rest should go to someone else this week.

2This week

  • Set a weekly order quota. When the quota fills, don't close sales; open a waiting list. Don't turn the interest away, put it in line.
  • Before outsourcing production, break the work into parts. Attaching chains, polishing and boxing are jobs that need no craftsmanship; give them to temporary workers and let the craftspeople do only the engraving.
  • Bring in temporary help for packing and answering messages. Write ready answers to the five most frequently asked questions; messages should go to the team first, not to the founder.
  • Don't drop the handwritten note, change its format: print the note the founder wrote, and have the person who prepared the package write their own name beneath it.

3Lasting fix

  • Train a second person who can do the final quality check. Every job that passes through one person stops the day that person falls sick.
  • Grow capacity not for a one-time wave but for the level of orders that will remain after the wave recedes.
  • Manage the waiting list like a valuable asset: announce new designs to them first.
The lesson

When every job passes through you, it looks like care while you're small; as you grow, it becomes the bottleneck.
