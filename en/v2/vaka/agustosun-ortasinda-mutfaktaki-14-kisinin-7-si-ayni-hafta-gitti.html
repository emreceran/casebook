TourismOctober 1, 2026

In mid-August, 7 of the 14 kitchen staff left in the same week

Sığla Koy Otel Tourism · Muğla · 55 employees · 64 rooms
The problem

With the hotel 94 percent full, half the kitchen team moved to the neighboring resort. Seven people were left to handle three meals and a 42-item menu.

How it was solved

Paid the accumulated overtime within a week, cut the menu to 24 items and closed the evening restaurant for nine days. Bridged the gap with three temporary cooks; by mid-September the team was back to 12.

What would you do? 3 comments ↓

What happened?

In the second week of August, seven of the 14 people on Head Chef Rıza Tuncel's team quit within four days. All went to the same place: the 420-room resort that opened ten kilometers away that summer had offered cooks 52,000 TL, while Sığla Koy paid 40,000 TL.

But it wasn't only about pay. Since early July no one in the kitchen had been able to take their weekly day off. When Pelin Arıkan looked at the records, she found that over the past six weeks an average of 62 hours of overtime per person had gone unpaid; 868 hours in total, about 232,000 TL.

The hotel is 94 percent full, and the kitchen serves three meals a day plus a separate restaurant in the evenings. Rıza Tuncel wants to shrink the menu and close the restaurant temporarily. Nesrin Karaöz, afraid of breaking promises to guests and of her scores dropping on review sites, wants outside staff brought in at any cost.

By the numbers

Kitchen team: 7 of 14 left, occupancy 94 percent
Monthly pay: Sığla Koy 40,000 TL, neighboring resort 52,000 TL (30 percent more)
Unpaid overtime: 868 hours, about 232,000 TL
Menu: 42 items, three meals a day and an evening restaurant

What did Sığla Koy Otel do?

Nesrin Karaöz spoke with the seven remaining staff that same evening; the 232,000 TL of accumulated overtime was paid within five days and the weekly day off returned. Rıza Tuncel cut the menu from 42 items to 24, and the evening restaurant stayed closed for nine days. Guests with reservations were called one by one, and two free grill nights were held on the beach. Three temporary cooks were brought in through an agency at 3,500 TL a day; over three weeks this cost 220,500 TL.

Kitchen salaries were raised to 46,000 TL and a 20,000 TL bonus was promised to those who stayed to the end of the season. Two of the seven who had left returned, and three new cooks were hired; by mid-September the team stood at 12. Guests' rating for the food dropped from 8.9 to 8.1 in August and rose to 8.7 in September. The hotel closed August 94 percent full, with no cancellations.

What to do in a similar situation, step by step

1First 24 hours

  • First, gather the seven who remain. Tell them you will pay the 868 hours of unpaid overtime within the week, and pay it; only this stops a second wave from following those who left.
  • Cut the menu the same day from 42 items to a number seven people can handle, and close the evening restaurant temporarily. 42 items served halfheartedly bring more complaints than 24 items served well.
  • Tell the guests about the situation yourself: leave a short note in the rooms, call those with restaurant reservations one by one, and offer something in its place.

2This week

  • Find temporary cooks: agencies in the region, city restaurants in their off season and trainees from culinary schools. It will be expensive, but it's for a three-week bridge.
  • Bring back the weekly day off right away. Make a rotating leave schedule even with seven people; a team working without time off will halve itself in two weeks anyway.
  • Call the seven who left. Find out who left for pay and who for time off and overtime; some may not have found what they were looking for at their new place.
  • Compare your pay with the neighbor's offer and calculate how much of the gap you can close. If you can't close all of it, offer a bonus to those who stay until the end of the season.

3Lasting fix

  • Pay overtime with the payroll at the end of the month, and have the head chef report the team's leave status every week.
  • Announce the end-of-season bonus in writing in April, when people start work. A promise made in August looks like bargaining; one made in April looks like a plan.
  • Design the menu around the staff: how many dishes can be served with how many people, and which dishes drop out when one person is missing, should be known in advance.
The lesson

A competitor only opens the door; what walks the employee to that door is often unpaid overtime and days off not given.
