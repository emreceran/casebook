Food cost rose from 31% to 38% in six months

Fırın Sokak Food & Drink · Izmir · 120 employees · 6 branches, central kitchen
The problem

Revenue is growing, but no money is left in the till. The seven-point rise means 980,000 TL a month, and the cause isn't in one place.

How it was solved

It made no across-the-board price rise; it fixed the four items carrying most of the loss and standardized portions with scales. The ratio fell to 33% in three months.

What would you do? 3 comments ↓

What happened?

Sales at Fırın Sokak's six branches are up from last year, but profit melts away by month end. When Operations Director Nalan Yücel looked at the numbers, she saw the cause: the ratio of food cost to revenue has risen from 31% to 38% in six months.

On monthly revenue of 14 million TL, seven points is 980,000 TL a month. Menu prices haven't changed in five months; over the same period, the price of meat rose 40%.

And the problem isn't only price. The same casserole comes out with 180 grams of meat at one branch and 230 grams at another. Discarded and spoiled products are written down nowhere. Head Chef Okan Bulut says customers will notice if the portions are touched.

By the numbers

Food cost ratio: from 31% to 38%
Monthly revenue: 14 million TL, loss: 980,000 TL a month
Meat price increase: 40%, menu unchanged for 5 months
Portion difference between branches on the same item: 50 grams

What did Fırın Sokak do?

The 15 best-selling items were re-costed at current prices. Four items turned out to carry 60% of the loss. The price was corrected on two and the recipe on two; the rest of the menu was left alone.

Scales and measured ladles were placed in every branch, and portions were brought down to the grammage in the recipe. Once discarded items began to be logged, it emerged that 140 kilograms of overprepared vegetables a week were going into the trash at the central kitchen. At the end of three months, the food cost ratio had fallen to 33%.

What to do in a similar situation, step by step

1First 24 hours

  • Before deciding on a price rise or a cut, find where it is leaking. Recalculate the recipe cost of the 15 best-selling items at today's purchase prices.
  • Often the picture turns out like this: three or four items carry most of the loss. Focusing on those items instead of raising the whole menu by 10% is both more effective and less visible.

2This week

  • Divide the menu into four: sells well and profitable, sells well but unprofitable, sells poorly but profitable, sells poorly and unprofitable. Fix the price or the recipe in the second group, promote the third group on the menu, and remove the fourth.
  • Don't shrink portions, standardize them. Put scales and measured ladles in every branch; bringing the branch that serves 230 grams down to 200 is not shrinking, it is following the recipe.
  • Start a discard log at every branch: what was thrown away, how much, why. A loss that isn't written down can't be managed.

3Lasting fix

  • Update recipe costs every month and automatically bring any item whose cost ratio crosses a set threshold onto the agenda.
  • For each branch, compare the consumption that should have occurred with actual consumption. The gap is portioning, waste or loss, and it varies from branch to branch.
  • On main items (meat, oil, flour), sign three-month fixed-price agreements with suppliers; review menu prices over the same periods.
The lesson

Cost doesn't leak from one big hole but from a hundred small places nobody measures.
