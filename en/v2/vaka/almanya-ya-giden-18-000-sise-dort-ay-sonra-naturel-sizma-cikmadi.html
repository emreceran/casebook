18,000 bottles sent to Germany did not test as “extra virgin” four months later

Oleícola Montesira Food · Jaén, Spain · 38 employees · 412 farmer members, 2,400–3,100 tonnes of olive oil a year
The problem

In a tasting analysis commissioned by the importer, the oil fell to a lower grade. The sample Montesira kept is still extra virgin; the difference lies in the clear bottle the customer asked for.

How it was solved

It took back the 11,800 bottles in the warehouse and sent new ones in dark glass, selling the returned oil in bulk as a lower grade. Of the 41,300 euro cost of the replacement, 9,010 euros stayed with Montesira.

What would you do? 3 comments ↓

What happened?

In February Montesira sent 18,000 half-litre bottles of extra virgin olive oil to a gourmet food importer in Germany, at 5.80 euros a bottle, 104,400 euros in all. The invoice was paid in April. In June an email arrived from the importer: a tasting analysis run on bottles taken from the warehouse had found a rancidity defect in the oil, and it had come out not as “extra virgin” but as “virgin,” one grade lower. The importer wanted the price of the 11,800 bottles it still held, plus shipping costs, refunded. The remaining 6,200 bottles had long since been sold.

Andrés Cobo, Head of Mill and Quality, pulled out the analysis done before shipment: the oil was extra virgin that day. A sample taken from the same tank and kept dark and cool was sent back to the tasting panel in June; the result was extra virgin again. The oil was the same; what had changed was the packaging: the importer had asked for clear glass bottles so that the colour would show on the shelf. Montesira normally used dark glass, and the bottles had sat for four months in the importer's well-lit warehouse.

Managing Director Rafael Molinero did not accept responsibility: “They asked for the clear bottle, and we delivered sound goods.” Lucía Barranco, Head of Sales and Export, went through the order correspondence. Montesira had nowhere put the risk of the clear bottle in writing, and on the label it had printed the usual 18-month best-before date. The importer was the only large customer in three years of work in Germany.

By the numbers

Shipment: 18,000 bottles at 5.80 euros each, 104,400 euros in total
Left in the importer's warehouse: 11,800 bottles (68,440 euros); already sold: 6,200 bottles
Pre-shipment analysis and kept sample: extra virgin
Bottle: clear glass at the customer's request; shelf life on the label 18 months

What did Oleícola Montesira do?

Lucía Barranco went to Hamburg with the analysis results and saw the importer's warehouse; the bottles were on shelves under the roof windows. The two sides agreed to replace the goods. In July Montesira sent 11,800 bottles from the same harvest in dark glass and took back the clear bottles. The oil, bottles, filling and two-way shipping came to 41,300 euros. Because the importer had asked for the clear bottle itself, it took on 14,000 euros of that.

The 5,900 litres of oil that came back were emptied from the bottles and sold in bulk in the virgin grade at 3.10 euros a litre; 18,290 euros came back. Montesira's share came to 9,010 euros. No complaint had reached the importer about the 6,200 bottles already sold, so these bottles were not recalled. In the new contract the clear bottle was dropped, and a 30-day period for quality disputes and a joint laboratory were written in. In September the importer placed a pre-order of 22,000 bottles from the new harvest.

What to do in a similar situation, step by step

1First 24 hours

  • Answer the importer with data, not with the contract: the shipment analysis, the new result on the kept sample, and an explanation showing that the difference came from light. Don't accuse anyone, just explain what happened.
  • Stop selling the 11,800 bottles in the warehouse together. Oil labelled extra virgin that isn't extra virgin reaching the shelf damages both your names, and in an inspection the fine goes to the importer.

2This week

  • Split the responsibility in two. The clear bottle was the customer's wish; but you are the one who knows how quickly olive oil spoils in light, and you didn't put that in writing, and you printed 18 months on the label.
  • Offer to replace the goods rather than refund the money: 11,800 bottles from the same harvest, in dark bottles. What the importer wants is not its money back but extra virgin oil it can sell.
  • Don't waste the oil that comes back. Oil in the virgin grade sells in bulk, and part of the cost of the replacement comes back from that.
  • Talk about the 6,200 bottles already sold. Have there been complaints, which shops did they go to, do they need to be pulled from shelves? Ask this question yourself before the importer does.

3Lasting fix

  • For a customer who asks for clear bottles, give the storage conditions and a shortened shelf life in writing; if they don't accept, don't sell in that bottle.
  • Keep a sample from every shipment and write a dispute period into the contract: quality complaints must be made within 30 days of arrival, on the result of a jointly agreed laboratory.
  • See an export customer's warehouse and shelf at least once. How the oil is stored after it leaves the mill is part of the product too.
The lesson

Doing what a customer asks doesn't free you of responsibility; if you said yes without a warning to a request whose risk you knew, you carry a share.
