Price fixed in euros, costs up 14 percent: profit margin fell from 10% to 1.3%

Saruhan Tekstil Textiles · Denizli · 210 employees · 40 looms, 12 million euros in annual exports
The problem

In January Saruhan gave all its buyers a one-year fixed euro price. Since then the exchange rate has risen 4% and lira costs 14%; three shipping months remain until year-end.

How it was solved

The company worked out profitability by product and went to the buyers with a cost table and a three-option proposal. Four buyers, accounting for 80% of revenue, accepted the 5% adjustment.

What happened?

Every January, Saruhan signs a one-year price list with its buyers. This year's list was calculated with a 10% profit margin: out of 100 lira of sales, 90 lira was cost. From January to September the euro rate rose only 4%. In the same period, lira costs, including labor, energy and yarn, rose 14%.

Finance Manager Nesrin Tuna brought the calculation to management. A sale worth 100 lira in January is worth 104 lira today, while the 90-lira cost has risen to 102.6 lira. The margin has dropped to 1.3%. On roughly 1 million euros of monthly shipments, profit was 100,000 euros in January and is 13,000 euros in September. At this rate of cost increase, October shipments will lose money. Orders to be delivered by year-end total 3 million euros.

Nevzat Saruhan's stance is clear: we signed, we gave our word; a manufacturer who asks for a price increase mid-year looks unreliable to the buyer, so we grit our teeth for three months. Nesrin Tuna says that shipping goods at a loss for three months means paying for it with a bank loan, and going into the January negotiation from a position of weakness.

By the numbers

Exchange rate increase from January to September: 4%, lira cost increase: 14%
Profit margin: from 10% to 1.3%
Monthly profit: from 100,000 euros to 13,000 euros
Orders to be delivered at fixed prices until year-end: 3 million euros

What did Saruhan Tekstil do?

Nesrin Tuna calculated the profitability of 240 products one by one: 38 products were already being sold at a loss, and most of them were the heaviest-weight bathrobes. Ceren Saruhan went in person to the two biggest buyers and spoke with the others by video call. A line-by-line cost table and three options were put on the table.

Four buyers, accounting for 80% of revenue, accepted a 5% price adjustment for shipments after November 1. One buyer left the price alone but cut the payment term from 90 days to 45; one buyer rejected both. Even though costs kept rising, the margin on November shipments climbed to 3.8%. The new year's price lists were made six-month lists, and a review clause for the minimum wage and yarn price was added.

What to do in a similar situation, step by step

1First 24 hours

  • Look at profitability product by product, not at the average margin. 1.3% is an average; inside it are products that still make money and others already losing money. This list decides which product you take into the meeting.
  • Write out the cost increase line by line: labor, energy, yarn, dye. Telling a buyer “costs have gone up” is a complaint; showing the table is a justification.

2This week

  • Go to the buyers with options, not a single request. Option one: a 5% price adjustment on last-quarter shipments.
  • Option two: the price stays the same, but the payment term drops from 90 days to 45. Your financing cost falls, and the buyer's price list doesn't change.
  • Option three: for the loss-making products, cut costs together with the buyer (packaging, pieces per carton, number of colors). A purchaser who can't change the price can often change the specification.
  • Go in person to meet the two biggest buyers; a price increase requested by email gets rejected by email.

3Lasting fix

  • Don't give a one-year fixed price. Make the price six months, or add a review clause that kicks in when the minimum wage and yarn prices pass a set threshold.
  • Track profitability monthly, by product and by buyer. It took eight months for the margin to fall from 10% to 1.3%; had it been spotted in the third month, the conversation would have been much easier.
  • For major inputs such as yarn, match the purchase price to the length of the sales contract: if you locked the sale for a year, lock the input too.
The lesson

Whoever fixes a price for a year must be able to fix costs for a year too; otherwise they pay the difference out of their own pocket.
