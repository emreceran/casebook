The biggest buyer cut orders by 40 percent, and 46,000 pieces sit in the warehouse

Saruhan Tekstil Textiles · Denizli · 210 employees · 40 looms, 12 million euros in annual exports
The problem

The German chain that brings in 37.5% of revenue cut its orders by 40% in a single email. The warehouse holds 310,000 euros of goods made only with that buyer's label.

How it was solved

Instead of offering a discount, the company asked for the reason: the buyer's own warehouse was full. Using the contract clause, it delivered 38,000 pieces at full price and found two new buyers for the freed-up looms.

What happened?

Of Saruhan's 12 million euros in annual exports, 4.5 million euros goes to a single German home textile chain. In early September the chain's purchasing department sent a short email: orders will drop by 40% going forward. That means 1.8 million euros a year in business gone. No reason was given.

Export Manager Ceren Saruhan had the warehouse counted the same day. Based on a three-month forecast the buyer had approved, 46,000 towels and bathrobes sit ready with the buyer's label and packaging, at a cost value of about 310,000 euros. A stockist wants the whole lot at a 45% discount; if that offer is accepted, a loss of about 140,000 euros will be booked. Of the 40 looms, 14 were working for this buyer, and six are about to go idle.

Nevzat Saruhan is afraid of losing the buyer. He is thinking of offering an 8% discount to win the orders back and quietly handing the warehouse stock to the stockist. Ceren Saruhan points to a clause in the framework agreement: the buyer must take delivery of goods made to its approved forecast within 120 days. In her view, a discount given without knowing the cause is money thrown away.

By the numbers

Buyer's annual orders: 4.5 million euros (37.5% of revenue)
Cut: 40%, or 1.8 million euros a year
Custom-made goods in the warehouse: 46,000 pieces, about 310,000 euros
Stockist's offer: 45% discount, a loss of about 140,000 euros

What did Saruhan Tekstil do?

Ceren Saruhan called the buyer's purchasing contact. The reason was not price: the chain's sales in Germany had fallen and five months of stock had piled up in its warehouses. The discount offer was dropped. The buyer was offered a drawdown schedule along with the contract clause, and it took 38,000 of the 46,000 pieces at full price within three months. The 8,000 pieces with only a custom label were relabeled and sold to another buyer at a 12% discount; the loss came to about 6,500 euros.

Buyers that had been quoted earlier were called again for the freed-up looms. Two chains, one Dutch and one Polish, placed trial orders totaling 520,000 euros a year. Less than a third of the cut could be replaced, but the German buyer's share of revenue fell from 37.5% to about 25%. Saruhan no longer produces labels and packaging before a firm order arrives.

What to do in a similar situation, step by step

1First 24 hours

  • Find out the reason before offering a discount. Call the buyer's purchasing contact and ask one question: why did orders drop? Is it the price, or is their own warehouse full? The remedies for the two are opposites.
  • Don't hand the goods to the stockist. First gather the documents you have: the forecast the buyer approved, the production dates, and the 120-day delivery clause in the contract.

2This week

  • Ask the buyer for a written drawdown schedule for the 46,000 pieces in the warehouse. Present the clause as a plan, not a threat: the goods are ready, so how much will you take each month?
  • Split the stock in two: pieces with the logo woven in, which can't be sold to anyone else, and pieces where only the label is custom. The second group can be relabeled and sent to another buyer.
  • Call back the buyers you quoted but failed to win over the past two years to fill the six idle looms. When capacity is empty, it is easy to say yes to small trial orders.
  • Run the numbers on the 8% discount: on the remaining 2.7 million euros of business it comes to 216,000 euros a year, with no guarantee it will bring the orders back.

3Lasting fix

  • Cap any single buyer's share of revenue (say, at 25%) and tie the sales team's targets to the number of new buyers.
  • In private-label production, separate forecast from firm order: weave only the greige fabric before a firm order arrives, and do the labels and packaging once it does.
  • Ask your big buyers about their store sales and stock levels every quarter. An order cut is often visible in their warehouse months in advance.
The lesson

When a buyer cuts orders, you ask why before you offer a discount.
